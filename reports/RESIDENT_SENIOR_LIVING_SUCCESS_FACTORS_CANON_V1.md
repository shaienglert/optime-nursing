# Resident–Senior Living Success Factors Canon v1

Status: RESEARCH CANON — NOT PRODUCTION SCORING

Scope: adjustment and sustained fit after an older adult or couple enters assisted living, nursing home, residential aged care, or a continuing-care setting. This canon does not authorize numeric success probabilities or provider ranking weights.

## Outcome Model

Placement success must not be reduced to one outcome. The evidence supports tracking at least:
- psychological adjustment / relocation adaptation
- resident-reported quality of life
- resident satisfaction
- family satisfaction
- autonomy / perceived control
- social connection / loneliness
- sense of home / belonging
- safety and avoidable adverse outcomes
- continuity and avoidable further relocation
- function / rehabilitation outcomes when recovery is the placement goal

## Key Evidence Table

| Evidence | Design / Sample | Findings relevant to Canon | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yong, Lin & Xiao, 2021, International Journal of Nursing Studies, PMID 33161332 | Systematic review; 20 studies (17 cross-sectional, 3 longitudinal) | Six factor categories; review specifically highlights decision-making control, self-efficacy, social support, ADLs and length of stay as important to adjustment. | Mostly cross-sectional; 19/20 studies moderate quality; many different adjustment measures. |
| Lee, 2010, International Journal of Nursing Studies, PMID 20138277 | Cross-sectional; 156 residents, 7 nursing homes | Self-efficacy, self-rated health, preconceptions, emotional support from staff/residents, family satisfaction and facility satisfaction predicted adjustment; model R² = 60.6%. | One country / subsidized homes; cross-sectional prediction is not prospective causal validity. |
| Gilbert et al., 2015, Healthcare, PMID 27417795 | Integrative review; 16 retained articles | Understanding the need for relocation and participating in the decision were repeatedly associated with fewer negative transition outcomes than forced relocation. | Majority qualitative; not a pooled effect-size estimate. |
| McCabe et al., 2021, Journal of Applied Gerontology, PMID 33402014 | 604 residents, 33 nursing homes | Choice and relationships accounted for 25% of QoL variance; choice over socializing and staff-resident relationship were significant contributors. | Observational; specific cultural/system context. |
| Xu, Kane & Shamliyan, 2013, Arch Gerontol Geriatr, PMID 23623273 | Systematic review; 11 eligible studies (1 longitudinal quasi-experimental, 10 cross-sectional) | Some evidence for nonprofit status, rural setting/private rooms in specific domains, and individualized Green House care; staffing quantity was not consistently related to resident-reported QoL. | Heterogeneity prevented meta-analysis; facility structure effects weak/inconsistent. |
| Cummings et al./StaRQ, 2024, PMID 38634535 | Mixed-method multi-work-package study including longitudinal provider data and evidence syntheses | Quality/person-centred care linked to managerial/staff stability and consistency, adequate staffing for relationships, skills, supportive leadership and autonomy to act. | UK care-home context; not a single numerical causal estimate. |
| Family Satisfaction With Nursing Home Care, PMID 26534835 | 16,790 family interviews; 13,433 resident QoL surveys; 376 facilities | Family satisfaction domains: care, staff, environment, food. Larger size, more deficiencies and chain affiliation associated with lower family satisfaction; resident QoL weakly predicted family satisfaction. | Family satisfaction is distinct from resident QoL; facility-level observational design. |
| Quality of life in assisted living homes, PMID 10794190 | 201 residents, 55 assisted-living facilities | Social climate cohesion strongest predictor across depression/life/facility satisfaction regressions; social activity, family contact and low conflict also predicted outcomes. | Older cross-sectional study; California setting. |
| Factors influencing sense of home, PMID 27313892 | Systematic review; 17 mainly qualitative studies | Recognition, habits/values, autonomy/control, coping, staff/resident/family relationships, activities, private space, belongings, outdoor/location features shape sense of home. | Primarily qualitative; mechanisms rather than predictive coefficients. |
| Gan et al., 2025, Age and Ageing, PMID 40996140 | Qualitative systematic review/meta-synthesis; 19 studies, 1,368 residents | Four domains: psychological, social, built environment and organizational. Autonomy, meaningful social connections and personalized environments recur as ideal home-like qualities. | Cognitively intact residents; qualitative evidence. |
| Gardiner et al., 2020, Age and Ageing, PMID 32396600 | Systematic review/meta-analysis; 13 articles, 5,115 participants | Estimated moderate loneliness prevalence 61% (95% CI 0.41–0.80); severe loneliness 35% (95% CI 0.14–0.60). | Prevalence evidence, not a placement predictor by itself; substantial heterogeneity. |
| Fealy et al., 2024, Aging & Mental Health, PMID 38634443 | Systematic scoping review; 8 intervention articles | Transition is distressing; identified post-move interventions but found no interventions implemented before transition, revealing a major evidence gap for pre-move preparation. | Scoping review; not effect-size synthesis. |
| Dementia transition review, PMID 23425369 | Review; 49 relevant articles | Adjustment difficult for residents/families; literature supports decision input, orientation, buddy-type support and person-centred approaches as plausible transition supports. | Authors explicitly note insufficient longitudinal research on what drives different outcomes. |
| Involuntary relocation study, PMID 17027625 | Prospective quasi-experimental secondary analysis; 120 residents, 56 family members, 23 homes | Falls increased after involuntary transfer; mobility/transfer assistance needs predicted post-transfer falls. | Closure/transfer context, not elective first placement. |
| Moving Our Care Home, PMID 35437921 | Longitudinal qualitative; baseline and 10–12 month follow-up | Family support and continuity of care helped settling; environment, size, staff organization and management change shaped adaptation. | Small qualitative sample; mechanism evidence. |

## What the Canon Rejects

### No universal 'best facility' score
Resident QoL studies show facility structural variables do not behave consistently enough to justify a simple generic quality score. Safety/clinical quality and personal fit must remain distinct.

### No raw staffing-number shortcut
Staffing matters for care quality and safety, but the literature repeatedly warns that staffing quantity alone misses turnover, consistency, skill mix, leadership, agency use and ability to build relationships.

### No demographic desirability variables
Age, race, ethnicity, nationality, religion, sex/gender, disability and other protected traits cannot be turned into desirability weights. Measure the legitimate underlying need directly (language needed for communication, care capability, dietary requirement, accessibility, privacy or culturally relevant daily-life requirement).

## Resident / Couple / Family / Facility / Transition Data Matrix

| Domain | Minimum variables worth collecting | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resident | care level/ADLs, mobility, cognition, mental-health transition risk where appropriate, preferences, routines, autonomy priorities, social style, activities/meaning, food/diet, privacy, location/visit preferences | supports clinical capability, preference congruence, adjustment and social-fit constructs |
| Couple | moving together or separated, each partner's care level, desired proximity, room/privacy needs, shared routines, escalation plan if one partner's needs change | evidence is weak but operational risk is material; must remain explicit rather than inferred |
| Family | visit frequency feasibility, involvement expectations, communication/reporting preferences, decision participation, concerns about move, support during first weeks | family support and staff-family relationship recur across evidence |
| Facility | verified clinical capabilities, staffing quantity, turnover/stability when available, inspections/deficiencies, complaints, safety outcomes, room types, personalization rules, activities, dining, social environment evidence, management continuity | separates capability/safety from lifestyle/person fit |
| Transition | resident participation, voluntariness, orientation/preparation, timing/urgency, prior moves, first-week support plan, continuity of staff/care, expectation gaps | transition process is repeatedly associated with adjustment and distress |

## Version-1 Factors

The agent recommends that OPTIME v1 measure these constructs explicitly:
1. Clinical Capability Fit
2. Decision Participation / Move Voluntariness
3. Transition Preparation / Expectation Realism
4. Functional & Cognitive Care-Needs Fit
5. Autonomy / Choice Fit
6. Preference Congruence / Person-Centred Daily Life
7. Resident–Staff Relationship Capability (using verified proxies and later outcomes, not invented ratings)
8. Family Connection / Visitability
9. Family–Staff Communication Expectations
10. Social Connection / Engagement Fit
11. Social Climate evidence where available
12. Sense-of-Home / Privacy / Personalization Fit
13. Staffing Stability / Consistency
14. Staffing Sufficiency / Skill Mix as safety/care capability
15. Verified Facility Quality / Safety History
16. Multiple-Relocation / Continuum-of-Care Risk

These are not numeric weights. They are canonical dimensions for data collection, evidence, next-best-question selection, and future validation.

## Research-Only / Do Not Weight Yet

- Facility size as an independent success factor
- ownership/profit/chain status as an independent success factor
- private room as a generic quality factor rather than a resident preference
- geographic distance as an independent success predictor rather than a determinant of family contact
- generic personality matching without validated placement evidence
- couple-fit score: collect couple variables, but direct outcome evidence is currently insufficient
- any universal 'probability of successful placement'

## Couple Evidence Gap

The literature is materially weaker for couples than for individual residents. Existing spouse-transition studies often examine the case where one partner enters a nursing home and the other remains outside, documenting loneliness, grief, guilt, relief, role change and the importance of support. This does not validate a predictive co-placement model for couples moving together. Therefore couple continuity, shared routines, co-residence, differing care levels and separation risk are operationally important but remain RESEARCH-ONLY until prospective outcome data are available.

## Next Research Questions

1. Which pre-move measures of decision control, expectation realism and self-efficacy prospectively predict 30/90/180-day adjustment?
2. Which publicly observable facility features validly predict resident-reported social climate and staff-relationship quality?
3. Can Texas/CMS staffing turnover, deficiencies, hospital/ED outcomes and complaint data improve prediction of avoidable relocation without simply proxying resident acuity?
4. What facility characteristics predict family satisfaction independently of resident QoL?
5. What are the best validated short instruments for autonomy, social connection, sense of home and adjustment in long-term care?
6. What predicts success when couples move together, especially when care levels diverge?
7. How much incremental predictive value does preference congruence add beyond clinical capability and facility quality?
8. Which first-30-day outcomes should OPTIME collect to learn without waiting 12 months?

WHAT is sufficiently defined to begin designing the data model and next-best-question logic, but NOT scoring weights. Production matching must preserve UNKNOWN and must not present a placement-success probability until prospective OPTIME outcome validation is available.
